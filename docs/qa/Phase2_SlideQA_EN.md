# ScrollGuard Phase 2: Likely Questions per Slide

Questions the audience may ask on each slide of the presentation (17 slides), with answers. Each answer starts with a line that can be said aloud. Slides marked **Watch out** contain a point that is easy to challenge; the note explains how to answer honestly.

---

## Slide 1. Title

**Q. What does Phase 2 deliver, in one sentence?**
A benchmark that tests whether an agent can tell mindless scrolling from intentional use, and step in only when needed. It comes with 81 test cases, labels checked by two people, metrics with success criteria, and scoring scripts, and it has been run on two real models.

---

## Slide 2. The task

**Q. Why three actions and not a simple yes/no?**
Because the product has three behaviors. Leaving use alone and stepping in gently are the two decisions. Asking first covers the cases where the signals conflict. A yes/no task would force a guess exactly where a careful agent should hold back.

**Q. Why write the reason before the action?**
Models generate left to right. If the action comes first, the reason can only justify a choice already made. With the reason first, the action can actually depend on it (lecture Ch04, "reason first").

**Q. Why is only the action scored, not the reason?**
The action is a closed set of three, so it can be scored automatically and reproducibly against the labels. Free-text reasons would need human or LLM judges. We keep the reasons for error analysis instead, and they revealed several problems (slide 15).

**Q. Why is there no blocking action?**
The Phase 1 idea is that users step away voluntarily, not by force. The strongest action is a grayscale fade with a check-in, and the user can always continue.

---

## Slide 3. One case, end to end (S013)

**Watch out.** The reply on this slide is an example of a correct reply. It is not what the models said. On S013, Qwen answered `no_intervention` in all 3 runs, and EXAONE in 2 of 3. That is finding 1 on slide 12.

**Q. Is that the model's actual reply?**
No, it shows what a correct reply looks like. The real models mostly got this case wrong. Qwen said `no_intervention` in all three runs, reading the stated goal as proof of intentional use. We come back to it on slide 12.

**Q. How do you make sure the agent never sees the answer?**
The labels and case metadata are in separate files that the runners never load. An automatic leak check also scans the inputs for words that give away the label.

---

## Slide 4. Two tracks

**Q. Why split into track A and track B?**
To measure two abilities separately: reading the context when it is given (A) and finding it on your own (B). Everything else is identical, so a drop from A to B comes from information seeking. That is how we found the tool-order effect on slide 14.

**Q. Why at most four tool calls?**
Three tools, plus room for one extra statistics call, which covers a complete trajectory. The budget is part of the protocol, as the lecture says for agentic benchmarks. It is reported with the results, and a different budget would make a different benchmark setting.

**Q. Isn't assuming perfect sensing unrealistic?**
Yes, deliberately. Phase 2 evaluates the decision layer. Mixing sensing errors in would make it impossible to tell a bad decision from bad input. Sensing is Phase 3 work.

---

## Slide 5. Test cases

**Q. Which parts are real and which are written?**
Usage time, sessions, mood and typical usage come from real rows of a public dataset (5,777 rows). Swipe patterns, stated goals, user states and observation logs were written or generated by us. This is a stated limitation.

**Q. What do the kappa values mean?**
Cohen's kappa measures agreement between two raters beyond chance: 0 is chance level, 1 is perfect. The two annotators reached 0.68 with each other. Fleiss' kappa extends this to three or more raters: 0.71 across both annotators and the original labels. Both count as substantial agreement.

**Q. Isn't 81 cases too few?**
It is small: the accuracy confidence interval is about ±0.1. So we only draw conclusions from large differences. We chose depth over size: hand-written contexts, two human annotators on every case, and cases aimed at specific failures.

---

## Slide 6. Nearly the same usage time, opposite answers

**Q. Isn't this pair cherry-picked?**
It is one illustrative pair, but the pattern is general. Among the 34 cases whose answer is "do nothing", 14 have 30 or more minutes of use. A 30-minute rule interrupts all 14, which is the 41% on slide 10.

**Q. What do the models do on these two cases?**
They get the lecture case (S024) right in most runs, but miss the short-video case (S013). That shows the benchmark separates the cases the way intended, and that the models still have trouble with a goal they have drifted from.

---

## Slide 7. Blocking shortcuts

**Q. What does "first 45 cases" in the footnote mean?**
The first version of the benchmark had 45 cases. The 0.76 for the swipe-CV rule and the dwell figure were measured on that version. The values after the arrows are for the current 81 cases.

**Q. If a word classifier still reaches 0.63, isn't there still a text shortcut?**
Partly, and we report it. That classifier is trained on the labels themselves, so it is a diagnostic ceiling, not a competitor. Its remaining cue words carry meaning, such as "feed", "scrolled in a row", "searched" and "notes". Surface cues like negations and text length were removed.

---

## Slide 8. Phase 1 goals turned into measurements

**Q. Why does an unneeded question cost 0.8, almost as much as an unneeded intervention (1.0)?**
With our first cost weights, the strategy of always asking ranked first, because questions were cheap. Asking also interrupts the user. With 0.8 that loophole is gone, and we report a sensitivity analysis over four weight sets.

**Q. Why is false intervention the core metric?**
The Phase 1 promise is to leave intentional use alone. An app that keeps interrupting people while they study gets uninstalled, whatever its accuracy.

---

## Slide 9. Metrics and success criteria

**Q. Why is the false-intervention target 0.10?**
It is a product judgment: interrupting at most 1 in 10 intentional sessions. It is far below the 41% of the time rule and close to the 6% of the best rule, so an agent must be as careful as the rules while catching more autopilot use.

**Q. How do you measure consistency? What is pass^k?**
Track A runs every case three times at temperature 0.3. Consistency is the share of cases where all runs agree. The lecture's pass^k asks the same thing: does the agent succeed every time, not just once?

**Q. Why must all criteria hold at once?**
Goodhart's law: if one metric becomes the target, it stops measuring what we want. "Always do nothing" has a perfect false-intervention rate but catches nothing. Only the criteria together describe a good agent.

---

## Slide 10. Baselines

**Q. Why is B2 the "best rule" if a dwell-time rule reaches 0.57 and the word classifier 0.63?**
B2 is the best rule fixed in advance, without looking at the labels. The 0.57 rule is the best single cue chosen after looking at the labels, a shortcut check. The 0.63 classifier is trained on the labels, a diagnostic. That is why beating B2 alone does not prove an agent reads context. We also check the counterfactual pairs, false interventions and the reasons.

**Q. Where does the 41% come from?**
14 of the 34 cases whose answer is "do nothing" have 30 or more minutes of use. A 30-minute rule interrupts all of them: 14/34 = 41%.

---

## Slide 11. Results

**Watch out.** "Both beat the best rule (B2)" holds for the averages, but the difference is not statistically significant on 81 cases. Qwen's accuracy CI is [0.53, 0.72], and its lower end touches B2's 0.53. On the cases where the two disagree, Qwen (run 1) is right 14 times and B2 5 times: exact McNemar p = 0.06. For EXAONE it is 18 vs. 13, p = 0.47.

**Q. Is the gain over B2 significant?**
Not at the 5% level with 81 cases. For Qwen it is close (p = 0.06), for EXAONE it is not. We read it as a trend, and the more robust signals are elsewhere. False interventions differ a lot (EXAONE 0.36 vs. Qwen 0.10), and so do the counterfactual pairs (0.83-0.89 vs. 0.50 for B2).

**Q. What does "Agreed 65" mean?**
Scores computed only on the 65 cases where both annotators chose the final label. It shows how much a result depends on contested labels. The ranking does not change.

**Q. Why did track B run only once?**
Time: each track B case takes several model calls on a local machine. It is a stated limitation, and track B consistency is therefore not measured.

**Q. EXAONE track B has a high detect rate (0.78) but low accuracy. How?**
It intervenes a lot: half of the intentional cases (false intervention 0.50). A high detect rate is easy if you step in almost everywhere. That is why we never read detect rate without false intervention.

## Slide 12. Finding 1: same accuracy, opposite errors

**Q. Isn't this just because the models are small and quantized?**
Possibly. We tested two 4-bit 7B models, so we cannot separate size, quantization and model effects. The point here is about the benchmark: two agents with the same accuracy behave very differently as products. Only separate metrics for false intervention and detection reveal it.

**Q. What are 60% and 15% computed on?**
- **60%:** EXAONE intervened in 60% of the "do nothing" cases with no stated goal (15 cases × 3 runs).
- **15%:** Qwen caught 15% of the autopilot cases that have a stated goal (9 cases × 3 runs).

---

## Slide 13. Finding 2: almost no asking

**Q. Is ask_recall near 0 a flaw of the benchmark?**
No. With the same inputs, the annotators chose "ask first" 15 and 9 times, so the task is possible for people. The two agents asked 2 times in 486 decisions. That shows the benchmark has headroom; it is not saturated. The real weakness is the size of this category: 15 labels, 5 agreed by both annotators. We report it, and the next version adds more.

**Q. Couldn't a clearer prompt fix it?**
We tried (experiment 1). A sharper definition raised asking from 0/15 to only 2/15, and unneeded interventions from 0/8 to 3/8. By our rule set in advance, we rejected it. Even with the full context (slide 14), Qwen never asked.

**Q. Is this about small models too?**
We cannot tell with two 4-bit 7B models. The benchmark claim is that it measures a gap between people and the agents we tested.

---

## Slide 14. Finding 3: tool order

**Q. 30 cases and one run: is that enough?**
For this effect, yes. 13% to 100% is far beyond what one run on 30 cases could produce by chance, and the decision rule (at least double) was set before the run. Smaller differences in the same experiment, such as accuracy, are not interpreted.

**Q. Why didn't you shuffle the tool order from the start?**
We did not expect the order to matter this much. Now it is part of the protocol: track B results are reported with the tool order, and the next version shuffles it per case.

**Q. What does this say about the benchmark, not the model?**
A track B score depends on the harness, not only the model (lecture Ch03). A benchmark has to fix and report conditions like the tool order, or scores are not comparable.

---

## Slide 15. What building it taught us

**Q. If accuracy stayed the same after the column fix, why does it matter?**
Because the model was right for the wrong reason. It read session concentration as attention. Without reading the reasons we would never have seen it, and a different case mix could have turned it into errors.

**Q. What does re-parsing saved replies mean?**
Every raw model reply is stored. When we improved the parser, we re-scored the stored replies instead of running the model again. Only two track A conversations that had timed out were rerun.

---

## Slide 16. Limitations and next steps

**Q. Are the findings specific to small models?**
We cannot tell yet: both models are 4-bit 7B. The findings support claims about the benchmark, not about model sizes. Testing larger models is the first next step.

**Q. What is the hybrid design?**
Give the agent what it always needs up front (goal, observation log) and let it fetch only the expensive or occasional data (the touch log) with a tool. This follows the lecture's advice to combine fixed retrieval with agentic retrieval, and it removes the dependence on tool order.

**Q. Why keep the labels private?**
If other teams can see the labels, they can tune their agents to the test set, which is contamination (lecture Ch03). In Phase 3 we share the inputs and the format, and score submissions ourselves.

It is a public research dataset on PsychArchives, cited with its DOI. We use only aggregated usage numbers and mood ratings from its `micro_datasetA.csv`. All personal details in the cases (goals, states, logs) are written by us.
